## About the experiment

Normally, if part of a ruler rests on a table while the other part sticks out and you strike the overhanging end sharply, the ruler may jump upward or fall off the table.

But what changes if the part resting on the table is covered with a large sheet of paper?

> [!CAUTION]
> A strong strike can break a wooden ruler and create sharp fragments. Perform the experiment under adult supervision and keep your face and hands away from the possible path of fragments.

## Experiment

1. Place the ruler at the edge of a table so that part of it extends beyond the edge.
2. First, without paper, strike the overhanging end sharply and observe the motion.
3. Return the ruler to the same position.
4. Completely cover the part of the ruler on the table with a large sheet of paper.
5. Smooth the paper as flat as possible against the tabletop.
6. Strike the overhanging end of the ruler sharply again.
7. Compare the results of the two trials.

## How does it work?

Air surrounds us and continuously exerts pressure on every surface. When a large sheet of paper lies flat on the table, atmospheric pressure acts across its entire area.

The force produced by pressure can be written as:

$$
F = PA
$$

where $F$ is force, $P$ is pressure, and $A$ is area.

Although we do not normally notice atmospheric pressure, the total force it produces over a large area can be substantial.

When the ruler is struck **very quickly**, the paper would need to lift almost immediately against the effect of the air above it. This makes the part of the ruler under the paper difficult to raise.

As a result, the ruler may bend more strongly and, with a sufficiently strong strike and a suitable ruler, may even break.

> [!NOTE]
> The ruler is not held down simply by the “weight of the air.” Atmospheric pressure, the large area of the paper, and the very rapid strike are all important.

## What can you test?

Compare small and large sheets of paper. A larger sheet is usually harder to lift quickly.

You can also compare slow pressure with a sharp strike to observe why speed matters in this experiment.
